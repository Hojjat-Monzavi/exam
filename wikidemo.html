Key Clinical Summary: COPD is characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction. It is a leading cause of global mortality driven primarily by inhalational risk factors.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a disease state characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction, and its burden is substantial because it is common worldwide and a 3rd leading cause of death worldwide. This text does not provide specific treatment-burden data for Iran or the Middle East.

Predisposing Factors

1. ID

  • Age distribution: Although age has not a direct relationship with development of this disease, COPD usually presents after decade 4 of life. It is because of time needed for cumulative dose of inhalational risk factors (such as cigarette smoke, opium inhalation, occupational mineral dusts/ Biomass,…) to cause alveolar wall destruction in emphysema and or goblet cell hyperplasia in chronic bronchitis.
  • Sex distribution: Male predominance is usually seen due to higher prevalence of smoking and or occupational risk factors in men. This discrepancy is not observed in countries with high rate of smoking in women. Interestingly women are more susceptible to destructive effects of inhalational risk factors.
  • Race and place of birth: COPD is described as more common among individuals exposed to noxious environmental factors, but the sent text does not provide a specific race- or birth-related distribution for COPD.
  • Place of residence: The text notes that biomass fuels in rural areas of Iran are common environmental exposures, implying that residence in such settings can be relevant to COPD risk.
  • Occupation: Occupations with high burden of mineral (not organic) dusts can overwhelm scavenger capacity of alveolar macrophages and cause alveolar wall and or bronchial damage. Biomass fuel combustion is another common occupational hazard in middle east including Iran. Biomass is fuels made up of living organisms, such as dry woods, cow dung which is used as fire fuels for purpose of warming or baking especially in rural areas.

5. Family History

  • Polygenic or monogenic diseases: The text states that genetics can contribute to COPD, and it also mentions alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency testing in COPD.
  • Infectious diseases: Childhood infections can delay lung growth and put the individual susceptible to smoking hazards. Infections (viral and bacterial) are the main causes of disease exacerbations.
  • Diseases due to a shared environmental source: The text identifies cigarette smoke and biomass fuels as environmental exposures linked to COPD.

Clinical Manifestations

At the time cumulative dose of inhalational risk factors established COPD via alveolar destruction and/or goblet cell hyperplasia, the natural history of disease is a chronic one, characterized by chronic dyspnea on exertion, cough and wheezes. The chronic stable phase of COPD is usually intermixed by attacks of exacerbating symptoms (usually preceded by infections).

The natural history of COPD can only be slowed down by inhaler medicines.

Cough, sputum production, and exertional dyspnea are the three most common symptoms of COPD, and many patients have these symptoms for months or years before seeking medical attention. Gradual, insidious progression of exertional dyspnea reflects slowly developing airflow obstruction and worsening small-airway disease, which is why patients often adapt their activity before presenting.

Acute exacerbations of dyspnea, cough, wheezing, and sputum change represent episodic worsening of airway inflammation and are a major feature of COPD’s natural history. Worsening dyspnea during activities of daily living indicates advancing airflow obstruction and greater functional limitation, linking symptom severity to disease progression. Resting hypoxemia may develop in advanced disease and can require supplemental oxygen. Tachypnea, accessory muscle use, inability to complete sentences, cyanosis, and mental-status change indicate severe respiratory distress and may suggest the need for inpatient care or ventilatory support. A prolonged expiratory phase and expiratory wheezing reflect airflow obstruction, while barrel chest, poor diaphragmatic excursion, and hyperinflation reflect air trapping and increased lung volumes. Cachexia and weight loss may occur in advanced disease and are described as an independent poor prognostic factor. Hoover’s sign reflects chronic hyperinflation altering the vector of diaphragmatic contraction on the rib cage.

Chronology

COPD usually has a chronic, gradual, insidious onset over months to years, and patients may later experience acute exacerbations superimposed on this chronic course. The text specifically notes that many patients think the disease began with an acute illness, but careful history usually reveals earlier symptoms. The tempo is often progressive, with worsening dyspnea on exertion and increasing exacerbation frequency as airflow obstruction advances.

Paraclinical Tests

  • Spirometry: A reduced FEV1/FVC ratio (less than 70%) is the hallmark of COPD and indicates airflow obstruction.
  • Lung volumes: With chronicity of disease, total lung capacity, functional residual capacity, and residual volume may increase, reflecting hyperinflation and air trapping.
  • Diffusing capacity: In emphysema, DLCO may be reduced, reflecting alveolar parenchymal destruction.
  • Arterial blood gases and oximetry: These may show resting or exertional hypoxemia; PaCO2 elevation is not expected until FEV1 is markedly reduced, and hypercarbia has important implications when present.
  • Chest x-ray / chest CT: Increased lung volumes, flattened diaphragm, bullae, paucity of parenchymal markings, or hyperlucency (looking more black) suggest emphysema; chest CT is the definitive test for establishing emphysema and defining airway involvement.
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin testing: The text recommends testing for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency in all subjects with COPD or asthma with chronic airflow obstruction; a low serum level is the initial test, followed by Pi typing if low.

Differential Diagnoses

  • Asthma: COPD differs from asthma by its persistent airflow obstruction and chronic progressive course. Asthma is typically intermittent and trigger-related.
  • Bronchiectasis (BTX): BTX can be started from childhood, especially when caused by severe childhood pulmonary infections. Both can cause chronic cough and sputum, but COPD is defined by chronic airflow obstruction and often smoking-related exposure.
  • Congestive heart failure: Dyspnea from heart failure may cause air hunger or suffocation, whereas COPD often causes chest tightness, inability to get a deep breath, and exertional dyspnea.

Treatment Plan

Immediate Priorities

Critical Warning: Patients with severe respiratory distress, severe hypercarbia, acidosis, markedly impaired mental status, respiratory arrest, or hemodynamic instability require invasive ventilatory support. Hospitalization is suggested by respiratory acidosis, hypercarbia, new or worsening hypoxemia, severe underlying disease, or an unsafe home situation.

Goals of Treatment

The two main goals are to:

  1. Reduce respiratory symptoms and improve exercise tolerance
  2. Reduce future risk by preventing disease progression, exacerbations, and mortality

First-line Treatment

For stable COPD, treatment is guided by symptoms and exacerbation risk. Inhaled bronchodilators are core therapy, and patients are typically treated with inhaled beta agonists and muscarinic antagonists; they may be given separately or together. For selected patients, triple inhaled therapy with a long-acting beta agonist, long-acting muscarinic antagonist, and inhaled corticosteroid reduces mortality. Smoking cessation, oxygen therapy in chronically hypoxemic patients, and lung volume reduction surgery in selected emphysema patients have been shown to improve survival. Pulmonary rehabilitation improves health-related quality of life, dyspnea, and exercise capacity, and reduces hospitalization rates over 6 to 12 months. Patients should receive the influenza vaccine annually, and pneumococcal vaccines and vaccination for Bordetella pertussis are recommended.

Decision Pathway

  • If the patient has frequent exacerbations, inhaled corticosteroids are considered because their main role is to reduce exacerbations.
  • If the patient has eosinophils below 100 cells per microliter, the text states that benefit from inhaled corticosteroids is not seen, while benefit increases above 100.
  • If the patient has resting hypoxemia with saturation below 88%, or 89% with signs of pulmonary arterial hypertension, right heart failure, or erythrocytosis, continuous oxygen supplementation is indicated.
  • If the patient has severe emphysema with upper-lobe predominance and poor postrehabilitation exercise capacity, lung volume reduction surgery may help, but patients with FEV1 20% predicted and diffuse emphysema or DLCO 20% predicted are not candidates.
  • If the patient has very severe airflow obstruction, severe disability despite maximal medical therapy, and no major liver, renal, or cardiac comorbidity, lung transplantation referral is appropriate.

Monitoring and Follow-up

Response to therapy should be assessed, and the clinician should decide whether to continue or alter treatment. For exacerbations treated in hospital, maximal improvement in lung function is often achieved by 8 to 10 days. After hospitalization, outcomes remain serious because rehospitalization within 30 days and within 1 year is common, so follow-up is important.

Second-line and Referral

  • Theophylline: Not first-line therapy because of side effects and drug interactions, although it may produce modest improvements in airflow and vital capacity.
  • Roflumilast: May reduce exacerbation frequency in patients with severe COPD, chronic bronchitis, and prior exacerbations, but its symptomatic benefit is modest and adverse effects are common.
  • Azithromycin: Reduced exacerbation frequency in patients with a history of exacerbation in the past 6 months, but the text notes less benefit in current smokers.
  • Specialist Referral: Referral to a specialist is warranted when the patient has very severe obstruction, severe disability despite maximal therapy, recurrent severe exacerbations, need for hospitalization, or consideration of lung volume reduction surgery or transplantation.

Patient Education

  • Explain that COPD is a chronic disease with persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction, and that the main therapeutic aims are symptom control, prevention of exacerbations, and slowing progression.
  • Teach the patient that smoking cessation is a key intervention and that inhaled therapy is used to improve breathing and reduce exacerbations.
  • Warn the patient to seek urgent care for worsening dyspnea, severe distress, cyanosis, confusion, inability to speak in full sentences, or new severe sputum change, because these may signal an exacerbation or respiratory failure.
  • Tell the patient that improvement may be gradual, and that pulmonary rehabilitation and oxygen therapy are used when indicated to improve function and survival.
  • Use teach-back by asking: “Could you please explain to me how you are going to take your medications and when you should seek urgent care?”
